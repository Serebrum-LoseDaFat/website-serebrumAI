§ 00

Overview & Operating Principles

What this document is, what it isn't, and the five principles that hold the system together.

This SOP turns three tools into one pipeline-generating machine. Each tool has a job. The mistake most teams make is treating them as interchangeable — using Apollo for everything, or trying to run sequences out of Sales Navigator, or asking an LLM to act like a database. This playbook keeps them in their lanes.

The five operating principles

  • Precision over volume. At enterprise ACV, 300 well-researched accounts beat 5,000 sprayed ones. Every step optimizes for fit and depth, not list size.
  • Accounts before contacts. Lock the target account universe first. "Finding contacts" is only hard when the account list is wrong.
  • Multi-thread by default. Enterprise deals are 6–10 person decisions. Never run a single-threaded sequence at a tier-1 account.
  • Signal beats persistence. A timely message tied to a trigger event out-converts a perfect cold pitch by 3–5x. Build the signal layer.
  • Measure replies, not opens. Open rates are a noise floor. Track positive reply rate and meetings booked per 100 contacted.
Read this first

If you can only fix one thing about your current process, fix tiering. Sections 03 and 04 will save you more time than the rest of the document combined.

§ 02

Define the Ideal Customer Profile

If this document doesn't exist, stop everything else and write it. Nothing downstream works without it.

STEP 1 Pattern-match your existing wins
CRM Claude

Pull your closed-won deals from the last 18 months. Filter to customers who renewed, expanded, or referred. Ignore one-and-done deals — they pollute the signal.

  • Export deal data: company name, industry, employee count, revenue, geography, contract value, sales cycle length
  • Note the buyer persona that championed each deal (title, department, seniority)
  • Note the trigger event or "why now" for each deal if known
  • Paste the export into Claude and ask: "What patterns do you see across these wins that I should turn into ICP filters?"
STEP 2 Document firmographic, technographic, and trigger criteria
Claude

The ICP doc has three layers. Don't skip the third — most teams do, which is why their lists look fine on paper and underperform.

Firmographic (who they are)

  • Industry: Specific NAICS or SIC codes, not broad categories
  • Employee count: Band that maps to your deal size (e.g. 500–2,500)
  • Revenue: Annual range
  • Geography: Countries, regions, sometimes metros
  • Funding stage / public status

Technographic (what they use)

  • Adjacent or complementary tools in their stack
  • Competitors they currently run
  • Infrastructure choices that imply readiness (cloud, modern data stack, etc.)

Trigger events (why now)

  • Recent funding round (Series B+ often correlates with budget release)
  • Leadership hire in the buying persona's department
  • Headcount growth in the relevant team (visible in job postings)
  • Public statements about the problem you solve (10-Ks, earnings calls, blog posts)
  • M&A, expansion, or platform consolidation announcements
STEP 3 Define personas with care-about statements
Claude

For each role in the buying committee, write a one-page persona doc:

  • Title variants and seniority signals
  • Top 3 things they're measured on this quarter
  • The problem you solve, framed in their language
  • The objections they raise first
  • What they need to see to say yes (evidence, social proof, calculator, peer reference)
How Claude helps here

Paste 5–10 LinkedIn profiles of people who hold this title at customer companies. Ask Claude to extract common career paths, language patterns in their job descriptions, and the publications / communities they engage with. That becomes your messaging vocabulary.

§ 03

Build the Target Account List

300–800 accounts for a year of prospecting. Not 50,000. Precision is the asset.

STEP 1 Draft filter strings with Claude
Claude

Before touching Apollo or Sales Nav, get the filter logic right. Paste your ICP doc into Claude and ask for:

  • Apollo company-search filter set (industry codes, employee bands, technographics, funding)
  • Sales Nav account-search boolean string (handles AND/OR/NOT and keyword fields)
  • Exclusion lists (competitors, current customers, do-not-contact)
Example — Sales Nav account search boolean (industry:"Financial Services" OR industry:"Insurance")
AND headcount:"501-1000"
AND (keywords:"data platform" OR keywords:"customer 360")
AND growth:>15%
NOT company:"[customer A]" NOT company:"[customer B]"
STEP 2 Run parallel searches in Apollo and Sales Nav
Apollo Sales Nav

Each tool sees a slightly different universe. Run the search in both and merge.

  • Apollo: save the search, export company list to CSV
  • Sales Nav: save as an Account List, export to CSV (or use the Account List + LinkedIn search alignment)
  • Deduplicate by domain in a spreadsheet (or paste both into Claude and ask for a merged, deduplicated list)
STEP 3 Layer signals to find "why now" accounts
Sales Nav Apollo

From the merged list, identify the subset showing buying signals right now. These become your priority accounts this quarter.

  • Sales Nav Account IQ: recent funding, leadership changes, hiring trends, news mentions
  • Apollo intent: third-party intent topics, website visitors (if Apollo Chrome extension is installed on your site)
  • Job postings: active reqs mentioning the problem space (massive signal)
  • News / SEC filings: ask Claude to scan recent 10-K or earnings transcripts for language matching your problem
§ 04

Tier the Account List

Different accounts deserve different effort levels. Tier explicitly or you'll over-invest in the wrong ones.

Tier 1
Strategic
  • Volume20–50 accounts
  • ProfilePerfect ICP fit + active buying signal
  • EffortManual research, custom messaging, multi-channel
  • OwnerAE + SDR pair
  • GoalBook meeting within 60 days
Tier 2
Programmatic
  • Volume200–400 accounts
  • ProfileStrong ICP fit, no live signal yet
  • EffortPersona-level sequences with light per-account personalization
  • OwnerSDR
  • GoalGenerate signal & book qualified meetings
Tier 3
Volume
  • Volume1,000+ contacts
  • ProfileICP-fit but broad; testing pool
  • EffortPure sequences, no personalization beyond merge fields
  • OwnerSDR / automated
  • GoalSurface intent, test messaging variants
PROCESS How to tier in practice

Score each account 1–5 on three axes and sum. Use Claude to do this at scale from a CSV.

  • Fit (1–5): How closely does this account match the ICP firmographics and technographics?
  • Signal (1–5): Is there a current trigger event suggesting they're in market?
  • Reach (1–5): Do we have warm paths, mutual connections, or existing relationships?

Total ≥ 12: Tier 1. Total 8–11: Tier 2. Total < 8: Tier 3 or drop.

§ 05

Map the Buying Committee

At $100K+ ACV, you're selling to a group of 6–10. Find all of them per account, not just one.

STEP 1 Define the committee template for your sale
Claude

For your product, document the standard committee shape. Most enterprise SaaS deals include:

RoleTypical TitleWhat They Care About
Economic buyerVP / SVP / C-level in the relevant functionROI, strategic fit, risk
ChampionDirector / Sr. Manager owning the problemSolving the painful day-to-day reality
Technical evaluatorHead of IT / Security / ArchitectureIntegration, security, scalability
End userManager-level practitionerWill this actually make my job better
InfluencerAdjacent leader (Finance, Ops, Data)Cross-functional impact
Procurement / LegalBuyer, CounselTerms, pricing structure, compliance
STEP 2 Pull contacts from Apollo by account + persona
Apollo

For each tier-1 and tier-2 account:

  • Search Apollo for the account, open the org chart
  • Filter people by department + seniority matching your committee template
  • Save 4–7 contacts per account (one per committee role minimum)
  • Verify email status — only "verified" or "likely valid"; never send to "guessed"
  • Export with: name, title, department, email, LinkedIn URL, location
STEP 3 Cross-check tier-1 contacts in Sales Nav
Sales Nav

For tier-1 accounts only — verify each contact is still active and look for warmth.

  • Confirm current role (Apollo data lags Sales Nav by weeks sometimes)
  • Check last activity — did they post or comment in the last 30 days?
  • Look for TeamLink intros (which colleagues are connected to them?)
  • Note any shared groups, alumni overlap, or mutual connections worth mentioning
  • Save the account to a Sales Nav Account List for ongoing signal monitoring
§ 06

Enrich, Verify, Prepare to Send

The work between "I have a list" and "I'm ready to send." Skipping this is why open rates collapse.

STEP 1 Second-pass email verification
Apollo External verifier

Apollo's verification is good, not perfect. For tier-1 contacts especially, run a second pass before sending. Bounced enterprise emails damage your sender reputation faster than any other mistake.

  • Apollo "waterfall" verification turned on
  • For tier-1 contacts, run through a secondary verifier (NeverBounce, ZeroBounce, or similar)
  • Drop anything below 95% confidence
  • Catch-all domains: mark for LinkedIn-first outreach instead of email
STEP 2 Pull personalization material for tier-1
Sales Nav Claude

For each tier-1 contact, gather public material that gives you a real opener — not a "I see you went to [school]" fake personalization.

  • Recent LinkedIn posts they wrote (their views, not corporate)
  • Posts they engaged with (what they care about right now)
  • Conference talks, podcast appearances, articles authored
  • Recent company news (funding, hire, product launch, restructuring)
  • Public statements about the problem you solve
Workflow

Paste the LinkedIn profile URL + recent company news into Claude. Ask: "Give me three personalization hooks for cold outreach to this person. Each should reference something specific they said or did publicly, and connect to [your value prop]. No flattery, no generic compliments."

STEP 3 Sync to CRM and sequencing tool
Apollo CRM
  • Push contacts to CRM with account, persona, tier, and source tags
  • Suppress against existing customers, open opportunities, and do-not-contact
  • Assign to the correct sequence based on persona + tier
  • Stagger sequence starts across the week — never send 500 emails on a Monday morning
§ 07

Messaging & Sequence Design

The same message to every persona is the most expensive mistake in enterprise outbound. Write one per role.

The three-layer message structure

Every sequence is built in three layers. Reuse the persona layer across accounts; rewrite the account layer for each one.

Persona layer (reusable)

  • The pain stated in their language
  • Your value prop framed for their KPIs
  • Proof point relevant to their role
  • A low-friction CTA

Account layer (custom)

  • The trigger event or "why now"
  • Mutual connection or shared context
  • Something specific to their company
  • An observation, not a flattery

Sequence anatomy (per persona)

1EmailHook + value prop + soft CTA60–90 words
2LinkedIn connectConnection request, brief note≤ 300 chars
3EmailDifferent angle, proof point40–60 words
4LinkedIn messageEngage with their content / share a resource2–3 sentences
5Phone (tier-1/2)Direct dial, leave a 20-second voicemail—
6EmailPattern interrupt — short, casual, low-stakes≤ 30 words
7EmailBreak-up — final note, leave the door open40 words
Claude as the sequence writer

Give Claude: the persona doc, the value prop, two or three real customer success stories, and the tone you want (consultative, peer-to-peer, etc.). Ask for the full 7-touch sequence with variants. Then have Claude rewrite each touch for a different persona in the committee.

Multi-thread coordination

For tier-1 accounts, run sequences to 3–5 personas in parallel, with deliberate sequencing of who hears from you first.

  • Week 1: Champion candidate (Director/Sr. Mgr) gets the first touch — they're most likely to respond.
  • Week 2: Add the economic buyer (VP+). Reference outreach to the champion: "I also reached out to [name] on your team."
  • Week 3: Add technical evaluator and end users. The internal conversation may have already started.
  • Always: Different angles per persona. Champion gets pain. Economic buyer gets ROI. Technical evaluator gets architecture.
Do not do this

Send the same templated email to six people at the same company in the same week. They forward it to each other within hours. The deal is dead before it starts.

§ 09

InMail & TeamLink — When to Spend Them

Sales Nav gives you scarce, high-value tools. Use them deliberately or you'll waste them on tier-3 prospects.

The hierarchy of LinkedIn touches

Most teams reach for InMail too fast. There's a ladder of options, each with different cost and conversion. Climb it in order.

Touch typeCostTypical reply rateUse when
Engage with their contentFree / timen/a (warm-up)Always. Comment thoughtfully on 1–2 posts before any direct outreach to tier-1.
Connection request (no note)Free30–40% acceptDefault for most prospects. Higher acceptance than noted requests in many tests.
Connection request + noteFree, ≤300 chars20–30% acceptWhen you have a specific, non-generic reason — mutual connection, their post, named trigger event.
Message after connectingFree10–20% replyOnce accepted, wait 2–3 days, then send a value-led message — not a pitch.
TeamLink intro requestSocial capital40–60% replyTier-1 only, when a colleague has a real (1st-degree, recent) relationship.
InMail (Sales Nav credit)1 credit, finite supply10–25% replyTier-1 prospect who hasn't accepted your connection request and isn't reachable by warm path.
Open Profile InMailFree10–25% replySame as above, but the prospect has Open Profile enabled — no credit consumed.

InMail — when, how, and how often

RULE 1 InMail is a tier-1 tool, never tier-2 or tier-3

Sales Nav Core gives you ~50 InMail credits per month. Sales Nav Advanced gives ~50 as well, with the ability to earn credits back from positive replies within 90 days. Either way the supply is small. If your team has 10 SDRs each burning credits on Director-level tier-3 prospects, you're spending real money to underperform a free LinkedIn message.

  • Tier-1 accounts only — typically the economic buyer or champion candidate
  • The prospect has not accepted your connection request after 7–10 days
  • You have a specific trigger event or "why now" to lead with
  • The email channel has stalled (no reply after touches 1 and 3)
RULE 2 Prefer Open Profile InMails first

Some prospects have "Open Profile" enabled — sending them an InMail does not consume a credit. Sales Nav indicates this on the profile. Always check before sending.

  • Sort tier-1 contact lists by Open Profile status when planning weekly outreach
  • Open Profile InMails should be your default LinkedIn-direct touch — they're free
  • Save paid credits for non-Open-Profile economic buyers who haven't accepted connection requests
RULE 3 Write InMails differently than cold email

InMail has its own format and norms. Treating it like a slightly shorter email is the most common waste.

Anatomy of a high-performing InMail

  • Subject line: ≤ 8 words, never a question, never "Quick question"
  • Opening: Reference something they did publicly within the last 30 days — post, talk, hire, launch. Specific.
  • Bridge: One sentence connecting their public signal to your value prop. No pitch.
  • Ask: Low-friction CTA. Not "15 minutes to chat" — instead "Worth me sending the 2-page summary?" or "Open to a reply if this resonates?"
  • Length: Under 100 words total. Under 75 is often better.
Draft with Claude

Paste the prospect's LinkedIn profile, their last 3 posts, and a recent company news item. Ask: "Write a 75-word InMail using [trigger event] as the hook. Tone: peer-to-peer, not salesy. End with a low-commitment CTA."

RULE 4 Track credit ROI weekly

Every credit is real cost. Treat them like ad spend.

  • Credits sent: per SDR per week
  • Acceptance / reply rate: target ≥ 15% on tier-1
  • Credits earned back: positive responses within 90 days return credits — track this as your "InMail efficiency" metric
  • Meetings per credit: the only metric that matters in the end

If an SDR's InMail reply rate is under 8%, pause their InMail sending. The issue is targeting or messaging, not effort.

TeamLink — the warm-path multiplier

RULE 1 Use TeamLink only when the relationship is real

TeamLink shows which of your colleagues are connected to a prospect. The connection's quality matters far more than its existence. A LinkedIn connection from a 2014 conference badge scan is not a warm intro — it's friction with extra steps.

  • Colleague has worked directly with the prospect, not just connected at an event
  • The connection is recent (within 3 years) or there's been actual engagement since
  • The colleague is willing to actually vouch for you, not just forward a name
  • The prospect is a tier-1 economic buyer or champion — never burn a warm intro on a mid-level contact you could reach cold
RULE 2 The two-step ask

Never ask a colleague "can you intro me?" Make it easy for them to say yes.

Step 1 — Permission check (to your colleague)

Internal Slack / email to colleague "Hey — I see you're connected to [Name] at [Company]. We're working on outbound to them — they fit our ICP and have [trigger event]. Would you be open to a forwardable intro? I'll draft the note so it's a 30-second forward for you."

Step 2 — Forwardable intro draft (you write it)

Draft you send to your colleague to forward "[Name] — wanted to put you in touch with [SDR/AE] from [Company]. They're working on [problem space] and I thought of you because of [specific reason tied to prospect's role/work]. [SDR/AE], over to you — [Name] runs [team] at [Company]."

Your colleague forwards the draft with maybe one line of context. Easy yes. The prospect now reads a peer-to-peer note, not a cold pitch.

RULE 3 Budget warm intros — they're not infinite

Your colleagues have a finite tolerance for being asked. Burn it on the wrong prospects and they'll stop saying yes.

  • Cap: No more than 1–2 intro asks per colleague per month
  • Close the loop: Always tell the colleague what happened — meeting booked, deal progressed, deal lost. This is the price of the favor.
  • Reciprocity: Track who in your company has connections to which target accounts. Build a "warmth map" so requests are spread, not concentrated on the two most-connected people.

Common mistake

Using InMail as the first touch on a prospect you've never interacted with. InMails work best as a tier-1 escalation after email and free LinkedIn touches haven't connected — not as a substitute for doing the cheaper, warmer work first.

§ 10

Reply Handling & SDR → AE Handoff

Replies arrive in three flavors. Treat each differently.

Reply typeExamplesSDR action
Positive"Tell me more" / "Send a calendar link" / questions about the productBook meeting within 24 hours. Confirm AE attendance. Send research note to AE before the call.
Soft / curious"Not the right time" / "Send info" / "I'll think about it"Respond with one specific value-add, ask one qualifying question, add to a longer nurture cadence.
Hard no / referral"Not me, talk to [name]" / "Remove me" / "We use [competitor]"Honor the request. If referral, restart sequence to the named person with the warm reference.

Meeting handoff checklist

  • Account brief sent to AE 24 hours before the meeting
  • Brief contains: ICP fit notes, trigger events, contact's recent activity, committee map, prior touches and replies
  • Ask Claude to draft the brief from your sequence log and Sales Nav notes — 5 minutes of work for the SDR
  • AE confirms attendance, prepares 2–3 hypotheses about the prospect's situation
  • Calendar invite includes a one-line agenda — never just "Intro call"

What to look at weekly

  • Reply rate by persona: If VPs reply at 3% and Directors at 12%, you have a messaging problem at the top of the committee.
  • Reply rate by sequence touch: Touch 4 should not have lower reply than touch 1. If it does, rewrite touches 2 and 3.
  • Subject line A/B: Always two variants. Kill the loser weekly. Replace with a new challenger.
  • Bounce rate: If above 3%, your verification process is broken. Stop and fix it before you damage domain reputation.
  • Tier 1 coverage: What % of tier-1 accounts have at least 3 contacts engaged this quarter?
Use Claude for reply analysis

Export your replies weekly (positive + objection text) and paste into Claude. Ask: "What objection patterns are emerging? What language is in the positive replies? What should I change in the sequence?"

§ 13

Anti-patterns

The mistakes that quietly destroy outbound programs. Audit yourself against this list quarterly.

Anti-pattern 01

"More contacts will fix it." If your reply rate is bad, adding 10,000 more leads makes it worse, not better. Fix the targeting and message first.

Anti-pattern 02

Single-threading at enterprise. One contact per account in a $100K+ deal is a leak. The buyer leaves, gets reorganized, ghosts — and the account dies.

Anti-pattern 03

Fake personalization. "I see you went to [school]" or "Loved your recent post" (which they never wrote). Worse than no personalization — signals you're a bot.

Anti-pattern 04

Sending without verifying. A 10% bounce rate on enterprise domains will get you flagged by Google/Microsoft. Recovery takes months.

Anti-pattern 05

Optimizing open rates. Apple Mail Privacy Protection has made open rates noise. If your team is celebrating 60% opens, they're not paying attention to the right thing.

Anti-pattern 06

Running Apollo and Sales Nav as duplicates. If you're paying for both and using them for the same thing, cut one. The point is to use each for its strength.

Anti-pattern 07

Asking Claude for contact lists. Claude doesn't have a database of real people with real emails. Asking will produce hallucinated names and fake emails. Use Apollo for who, Claude for how.
